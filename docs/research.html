Research

We discover quantum materials and build the autonomous tools to synthesize them. The crystal serves the physics: we design and grow large, high-quality single crystals of materials whose electrons do something interesting, and then we measure them, on our own 14 T cryostat and at neutron, muon, and synchrotron facilities around the world.

Autonomous crystal growth: AutoFlux

Flux crystal growth is opaque. The crucible hides the entire process, so the first look at any growth comes only after it has ended, and a failed run returns almost no information. Outcomes hinge on experience and intuition, growths are hard to reproduce between laboratories, and the primary literature records recipes, not processes.

AutoFlux is our answer: a platform, built in our dedicated growth laboratory, that images flux growth inside the furnace as it happens and uses those images to act on the growth in real time. The long-term vision is to close the loop between observation and control, so that flux growth becomes reproducible, documentable, and ultimately learnable by machines as well as people. The work is supported by the National Science Foundation.

What we study

Frustrated quantum magnetism

On triangular, pyrochlore, and diamond lattices the magnetic moments cannot satisfy all of their neighbors at once. Frustration suppresses ordinary order and can leave a material fluctuating and entangled down to the lowest temperatures. When the ion also carries an orbital degree of freedom, the question sharpens: does the orbital freeze, distort the lattice, or stay entangled with the spin? We look for the answer in oxides, fluorides, and sulfides, following their magnetism with magnetometry and heat capacity, their structure with neutron and synchrotron diffraction, and their dynamics with muon spin rotation and inelastic neutron scattering.

Superconductivity and competing orders

Superconductivity rarely appears alone. In the materials we care about it emerges next to magnetism, charge or orbital order, or a metal-to-insulator transition, and small changes in composition or structure tip the balance between them. We synthesize crystalline materials that sit near these boundaries, identify the structural and electronic parameters that move them, and map their phase diagrams with transport and thermodynamic measurements.

Correlated and topological metals

In metals with strong correlations, magnetic order, or nontrivial band topology, transport reveals what ordinary metals hide: anomalous Hall effects, nonlinear responses, and signatures of frustrated or topological states. We grow single crystals of layered and kagome metals and of Dirac and Weyl semimetals, measure them at low temperature and high field, and share them with collaborators whose device and spectroscopic studies need large, clean samples.
