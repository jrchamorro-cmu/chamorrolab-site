Research

We discover quantum materials and build the autonomous tools to synthesize them. The crystal serves the physics: we design and grow large, high-quality single crystals of materials whose electrons do something interesting, and then we measure them. Growth happens in our own laboratories in Wean Hall, from flux and from the melt, in sealed tubes and in gloveboxes. Measurement happens on our 14 T cryostat and at neutron, muon, and synchrotron facilities around the world.

Frustrated quantum magnetism

On a lattice of corner-sharing tetrahedra, on a triangular lattice, or on a diamond lattice, the magnetic moments cannot satisfy all of their neighbors at once. Frustration of this kind suppresses ordinary magnetic order and can leave a material in a fluctuating, entangled state down to the lowest temperatures, with fractionalized excitations and emergent gauge fields. When the magnetic ion also carries an orbital degree of freedom, the question sharpens: does the orbital freeze, distort the lattice, or stay entangled with the spin?

We look for the answer in oxides, fluorides, and sulfides built on these lattices, and in particular in materials where chemistry lets us tune how spin, orbital, and lattice compete. We synthesize them as powders and single crystals, and we follow their magnetism with magnetometry and heat capacity, their structure with neutron and synchrotron diffraction, including total scattering for local distortions, and their spin dynamics with muon spin rotation and inelastic neutron scattering. Several of the materials we study are candidate quantum spin liquids and orbital liquids. Others are testbeds for how entangled spin-orbital states form and fall apart.

Superconductivity and competing electronic orders

Superconductivity rarely appears alone. In the materials we care about it emerges next to magnetism, charge or orbital order, or a metal-to-insulator transition, and small changes in composition or structure tip the balance between them. A single structure type can host a charge-ordered insulator, a correlated metal, and a superconductor, with composition as the knob.

Rather than trying to solve the mechanism of superconductivity outright, we synthesize and characterize crystalline materials that sit near these boundaries, identify the structural and electronic parameters that move them, and use transport and thermodynamic measurements to map their phase diagrams.

Correlated and topological metals

In metals with strong correlations, magnetic order, or nontrivial band topology, transport reveals physics that ordinary metals hide: anomalous Hall effects, nonlinear responses, and signatures of frustrated or topological states near phase boundaries. We grow single crystals of layered and kagome metals and of Dirac and Weyl semimetals, and we measure their electrical and thermal transport at low temperature and high field. Many of these crystals go to collaborators at Carnegie Mellon and elsewhere for device and spectroscopic studies that need large, clean samples.

Autonomous crystal growth: AutoFlux

Flux crystal growth is opaque. The crucible hides the entire process, so the first look at any growth comes only after it has ended, and a failed run returns almost no information. Outcomes hinge on experience and intuition, growths are hard to reproduce between laboratories, and the primary literature records recipes, not processes.

AutoFlux is our answer: a platform, built in our dedicated growth laboratory, that images flux growth inside the furnace as it happens and uses those images to act on the growth in real time. The long-term vision is to close the loop between observation and control, so that flux growth becomes reproducible, documentable, and ultimately learnable by machines as well as people. The work is supported by the National Science Foundation.
